Bringing external engineers into a programme is usually a question of capacity. The risk is that it quietly becomes a question of control: decisions drift toward whoever is writing the most code, and the organisation ends up depending on people it does not employ.

That outcome is avoidable. In our experience, four practices make the difference.

## Keep architecture decisions in-house

Augmented engineers should contribute to design discussions, but decisions and their records belong to the client's own architects. A lightweight decision log, owned internally, is enough.

## Onboard to your standards, not ours

Augmented engineers should adopt the client's tooling, review process and definition of done from the first week. Parallel ways of working are the fastest route to a codebase only its authors understand.

## Pair across the boundary

Pair every external engineer with an internal counterpart on the work that matters most. Knowledge transfer then happens during delivery rather than in a handover document at the end.

## Plan the exit at the start

Agree from the outset what the client's team must be able to run alone, and track progress against it. A good augmentation engagement ends with a stronger internal team, not a renewal by default.
